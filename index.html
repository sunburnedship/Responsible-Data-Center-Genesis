Course module · AI infrastructure 2026

You have been hired as the COO to design, finance, and operate a 250 MW AI data center.

The world's data centers consumed roughly 176 TWh of electricity in 2023—about 4.4% of total US demand. By 2028 the same fleet may consume between 325 and 520 TWh, or 6.7–12% of US electricity. Capacity that once described a city now describes a single training cluster. Your task in this simulation is not to optimize one number. It is to make the dozens of coupled decisions that turn a data center from a spreadsheet into a permitted, powered, cooled, and politically tolerated machine that runs for ten years.

Every choice you make in Phase I creates the conditions you live with in Phases II and III. Cooling choice drives water use. Water use drives community sentiment. Community sentiment drives permitting risk. Permitting risk drives investor patience. Phase IV debriefs your performance across financial, environmental, social, political, water, and reliability dimensions and shows you the path you should have taken.

How the scoring works

Course rubric

At the end of the simulation you receive a letter grade in six dimensions. None of them is the whole game; an excellent operator must balance them. In real practice, weak performance in any single dimension can shut a project down.

Financial

Total project cost vs. budget, ongoing operating cost, revenue captured, IRR proxy. Penalties for unplanned overruns and pivot spending.

Environmental

Lifecycle CO₂ per MWh, total emissions over 5 operating years, and progress toward net-zero commitments your investors expect.

Social / Community

Local sentiment trajectory. Falls when residents see water draw, noise, light pollution, heat island effects, or a perceived lack of jobs/transparency.

Political & Regulatory

Permitting timeline overruns, zoning challenges, regulator confidence, and the risk of a forced shutdown or operating restriction.

Water Stewardship

Water Use Effectiveness (WUE) and absolute consumption relative to local water stress. Stewardship matters more in arid regions.

Reliability / Uptime

Annual availability across your 5 operating years. Outages, cooling failures, power purchase failures, and grid events all hit this score.

Real-world data center industry rules of thumb: PUE 1.10–1.30 for modern facilities (1.0 = perfect), WUE 0.1–1.8 L/kWh, target uptime >99.99% (Tier IV). These benchmarks set the rubric.

Phase I · Design & Site Selection

Capital decisions · Permanent commitments

In this phase you commit to the four decisions that lock in 70% of your future operating reality: where you will build, how big, what powers you, and how you cool. You may also decide your compute density and how much you will invest in the host community before construction.

1. Site region

Geography sets the rules

Each region carries a unique constraint envelope: power markets, water stress, climate, community attitudes, latency to customers, and political baseline. Some constraints are absolute (e.g., no nuclear permits in your jurisdiction); others are pricing or sentiment effects.

2. Capacity tier

Defines budget envelope

Larger campuses unlock economies of scale on both equipment and PPAs but face exponentially harder permitting and grid interconnection challenges. Sub-100 MW projects can sometimes piggyback on existing substations; sub-500 MW projects rarely can in 2026.

3. Compute architecture

Drives cooling and revenue model

Standard CPU/general workloads run at ~10 kW per rack and tolerate air cooling. AI training racks run at 80–130 kW per rack and require liquid cooling at the chip. The choice changes both your customer pool and your cooling and water requirements.

4. Energy procurement mix

Allocate to 100%

You may blend any combination of these sources. Some are gated by your region (e.g., no offshore wind in landlocked sites). Greyed-out sliders are unavailable. Cost figures are 2026 LCOE estimates including transmission and storage adders where applicable.

Allocation total: 0%
Allocate to exactly 100%

A 100% renewable mix without storage or firming exposure can score badly in reliability if your grid cannot support it. Mix design is a tradeoff, not an optimization.

5. Cooling system

Water vs. energy vs. capex

The single decision with the most cascading consequences in this simulation. Lower-water systems cost more upfront and use more electricity. Lower-energy systems may use significant water in evaporative phases. High-density AI compute (>40 kW/rack) cannot be air-cooled.

6. Heat reuse strategy (optional)

Optional partnership

Modern data centers reject 95%+ of input energy as low-grade waste heat (typically 30–55°C). Capturing it for district heating, agriculture, or industrial use earns community goodwill and can offset operating costs, but requires partner infrastructure.

7. Pre-construction community investment

$0 – $150M

Investments here include open community meetings, grants to the local school district, environmental impact studies beyond the legal minimum, and visible commitments such as scholarship endowments, water rights donations, or municipal infrastructure grants. Spending here is the cheapest insurance you will buy.

In real cases (Microsoft Quincy, Google The Dalles, Meta Eagle Mountain), pre-construction commitments of 0.5–1.5% of capex are typical for projects that landed without major opposition. Below 0.2% creates a high probability of permit delay.
